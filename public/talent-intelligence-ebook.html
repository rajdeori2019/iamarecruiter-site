Practical guide for working recruiters

TALENT INTELLIGENCE
BEFORE SOURCING.

This guide is about one shift: do not start with profiles. Start by understanding the requirement, what the market can support, what evidence you actually have, and what decision the hiring manager needs to make.

Chapter 1

Talent intelligence is not “asking AI for the market.”

AI can help structure a search, organise evidence and draft a recommendation. It does not automatically make candidate counts, salary data, company relevance or hiring feasibility true.

Rule: Fact, inference, assumption and unknown are different things. If evidence is missing, keep it as “Unknown — requires verification.”
Chapter 2

Diagnose the hiring requirement before you search.

A JD often mixes confirmed requirements, preferences, assumptions and language copied from older roles. Your first job is to separate them.

Ask:

  • Which 3 skills are genuinely non-negotiable?
  • Is domain experience essential or can adjacent experience work?
  • Is the title important, or is scope more important?
  • Is the experience range doing real screening work?
  • Are location, budget and joining expectations fixed or flexible?
  • Are we hiring one archetype or mixing multiple profiles?
A · Non-negotiableCandidate cannot perform the core job without it.
B · Strong preferenceUseful, but not essential.
C · Transferable / adjacentEquivalent experience may work.
D · UnclearNeeds a hiring-manager decision.
Chapter 3

Build search lanes, not one giant Boolean string.

For many difficult roles, one search does not represent the whole market. Separate a direct-fit lane from one or more adjacent-fit lanes.

Capture:

  • Primary and adjacent titles
  • Must-have keywords and synonyms
  • Transferable skills
  • Likely exclusions / noise
  • Direct-fit lane
  • Adjacent-fit lane
  • LinkedIn, Google X-Ray and Naukri / Resdex logic where relevant
A search architecture is a hypothesis. It becomes useful when you test it in real sourcing tools.
Chapter 4

Record what the market actually shows.

Do not rely on memory or a vague “market is tough” statement. Capture the exact search context.

PlatformWhere you searched.
DateWhen the observation was made.
GeographyThe location scope used.
Search / filtersWhat you actually searched.
Observed countOnly if the tool showed it.
Quality notesNoise, duplicates, weak matches, missing skills, or other issues.

Search-result counts are directional indicators. They are not automatically the true available talent pool.

Chapter 5

Map companies, locations and compensation with evidence.

Target companies

Use three pools: Tier 1 direct fit, Tier 2 adjacent fit, Tier 3 alternative pool. A company suggested by AI is a research candidate until evidence supports why it belongs.

Location

Compare the requested location with adjacent hubs, remote / hybrid options and relocation assumptions only when you have evidence. Do not invent talent-density claims.

Compensation

Use the strongest evidence available and record source, role, location, experience, compensation, date and limitations. Prefer employer-published ranges first, then candidate-reported / Resdex evidence, then sources such as Glassdoor or AmbitionBox where appropriate.

Chapter 6

Turn evidence into a hiring-manager recommendation.

The goal is not to create a long report. The goal is to help the hiring manager make a better decision.

01EVIDENCE
02IMPLICATION
03RECOMMENDATION
04HM DECISION

Example structure

Evidence: The recruiter-tested direct-fit search returns a small, noisy set and the adjacent lane produces more relevant profiles.

Implication: The current requirement may be over-indexing on one title or domain.

Recommendation: Expand the search to the validated adjacent lane.

HM decision: Confirm whether adjacent domain experience is acceptable.

Good recruiter advisory is not certainty theatre. If the evidence is incomplete, say what is still unknown and what you will test next.
